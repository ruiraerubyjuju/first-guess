First Guess

PRD summary

PRD v0.1 in eleven pictures, from the view of the product design engineer.

Overview

Help people decide, not browse.

A decision platform where people help each other make better decisions. Every question ends in one clear answer: the First Guess.

The core loop
  1. AskSomeone needs help with a decision
  2. AnswerCurators suggest options on open questions
  3. VoteEveryone votes; experts also form a separate Expert Vote
  4. First GuessOne clear winner the asker can act on

↺ Good decision → the asker comes back, and helpers build reputation and earnings

Problem to opportunity

Plenty of opinions, no decision.

Every tool people use today helps them find more options. None of them is built to help finish a decision.

SearchToo many options, ranked by SEO
AlgorithmsOptimise for engagement and ads, not your choice
AICan recommend, but lacks taste and lived experience
Social mediaPlenty of opinions, not structured to reach a decision
FriendsUseful, but busy and awkward to ask again and again
ReviewsAbout the product, not the context of your decision
↓
The opportunity

Turn human judgment into a structured decision resource.

  • Real people with taste and experience
  • Structured around one question
  • Ends in a clear answer, not a feed

One account, layered roles

One account. No fixed roles.

Everyone can ask, vote and answer. The names describe what someone does or knows, not a type of account.

ReputationEarned over time

Badges: Top Answerer (answers that win) and Good Taste (votes that match the experts).

ExpertStatus layer

Pick 1 category for free (more on paid). Your vote on that topic also counts in the Expert Vote.

CuratorBehaviour

Anyone who writes original answers to open questions. Can earn bounties and rewards.

First GuesserEvery user

Ask a question · Vote on options · Answer open questions

Built from the bottom up: everyone starts as a First Guesser. The layers above come from behaviour, so nobody picks a role at sign-up.

Two question types

Two ways to ask.

Closed questions collect votes on options the asker already has. Open questions collect new ideas first, then votes on those ideas.

Closed-ended

“Which of these three dresses should I wear?”

  1. Asker adds optionsA · B · C
  2. Everyone votesPublic Vote + Expert Vote
  3. First Guess: Option B

Free: up to 100 votes

Open-ended

“What's the best $200 birthday gift for my sister?”

  1. Curators submit answersAnswer #1 … #5
  2. Everyone votes on answersHighest vote wins, or Asker's Pick (paid)
  3. First Guess: Answer #3

Free: up to 5 answers · unlimited with a bounty

Anatomy of a First Guess

The result screen is the product.

Show the decision first and keep the raw content out of the way.

  1. Label the pick. Community Pick (top voted) and Asker's Pick (paid) must never be confused.
  2. Winner first. The option or answer, big and unmistakable.
  3. Public Vote. Broad community sentiment.
  4. Expert Vote. Relevant experts only, shown when available.
  5. Participation. How many people and experts took part.
  6. Reasoning. Why people chose it, kept short.
  7. Next step. Act on it. This is how Successful Decisions are measured.

How money moves

Two ways contributors get paid.

Bounties come from the asker for one question. Answer Rewards come from the platform for consistent quality. They are separate systems.

Bounty
  1. Asker“I'll pay $50 for the best answer.” Set before publishing; skips the free 5-answer limit.
  2. First GuessTakes a platform fee, handles payment, fraud and disputes
  3. Winning answerTop voted by default, or chosen by a paid asker
Answer Rewards Later
  1. Platform poolFunded by First Guess
  2. Eligibility barVolume, quality, votes, win rate, asker feedback, outcomes
  3. Top contributorsPaid for useful answers, not for the most answers
Where First Guess makes money
MembershipMore reach, winner control, unlimited answering. Tested with a fake door in the MVPLater
Bounty feeA cut of every bounty paid outMVP
AffiliateRevenue when people buy what's recommendedLater
BrandsPaid placement, always clearly labelledFuture

Free vs paid

Free stays useful. Paid buys reach and control.

Participation drives the whole network, so the free product can't feel limited.

FeatureFreePaid member
Votes received per question100More
Answers received (non-bounty)5More
Open answers you can submitLimitedUnlimited
Expert categories1Multiple
Bounty winnerTop votedAsker chooses

Both: ask, vote, earn bounties, badges. Membership comes later, not in the MVP. Until then everything is free, and the upgrade screen is a fake door ("coming soon, join the list") that measures who would pay. Pricing and exact limits are still TBD.

MVP scope and launch

Prove the loop before building the platform.

The first version can be partly manual. Complexity is added only once behaviour proves it's needed.

In the MVP
  • Accounts Sign up, profile, 1 expert category
  • Questions Closed and open, category, status, counts
  • Voting Public vote, expert tag, basic totals
  • Answers Submit, view, vote, find the top one
  • Bounty Set before publishing, pay, award winner
  • Upgrade test "Coming soon" prompt at 100 votes or 5 answers
  • Results First Guess, both votes, ranking
  • Moderation Report, remove spam, admin review
Not yet
  • AI matching
  • Social feed
  • Brand marketplace
  • Personalisation
  • Complex reputation maths
  • Automated expert checks
  • Anti-collusion algorithms
  • Native mobile apps
  • Creator analytics
Launch phases
  1. Phase 1Concierge20–30 real questions, 20–50 contributors, run by hand, gifting firstIs the First Guess genuinely useful?
  2. Phase 2MVP100+ askers, 100+ contributors, first paid questions and bountiesDoes it keep going without the team running every question by hand?
  3. Phase 3MonetiseMembership, bounty take rate, contributor rewardsCan it support real incentives?
  4. Phase 4ScaleMatching, AI synthesis, reputation, anti-fraud, brand marketplaceOnly after demand is proven

How success is measured

Success = decisions, not content.

The MVP doesn't win with a beautiful app or thousands of sign-ups. It wins if people want the collective judgment, help willingly, and some will pay.

North star metric

Successful Decisions

The asker gets useful recommendations and takes a real next step: picks an answer, clicks, buys, marks it resolved or says they're satisfied.

9 hypotheses to test, not assume
  1. H1 · Human judgmentPeople prefer human judgment over search or AI for some decisions
  2. H2 · SupplyPeople will answer strangers' questions voluntarily
  3. H3 · BountyA bounty raises answer quality or participation
  4. H4 · ExpertPeople care about public vs expert judgment
  5. H5 · ClarityOne clear First Guess beats a pile of opinions
  6. H6 · PaymentSome will pay for more votes and answers
  7. H7 · ControlSome will pay to choose their own winner
  8. H8 · RetentionGood contributors stay for money and reputation
  9. H9 · ReputationJudgment-based reputation beats follower counts

Highlighted: the hypotheses Phase 1 research tests directly in interviews and prototype tests.

What this means for user experience

Make the core loop easy and trustworthy.

Do that before any visual design, then prove it with real people.

Screens, in priority order
  1. First Guess resultThe core screen. Winner, both votes, reasoning.
  2. Ask flowType, category, options or prompt, bounty
  3. VotingFast, low effort, closer to swiping
  4. Answer submissionPrompts that push quality over volume
  5. Upgrade momentFake door at 100 votes or 5 answers: “coming soon, join the list”
  6. ProfileExpert category, badges, earnings
  7. OnboardingEveryone can do everything, no role jargon
Design tensions to solve
Public vs ExpertShow both clearly, even when they disagree.
Community vs Asker's PickLabel who chose the winner so trust holds.
Money vs trustBounties and brands must never look like expert judgment.
VocabularyFirst Guesser, Curator, Expert: too many terms for day one.

What got cut to fit 4 weeks

The first draft of this plan ran 6 weeks. To fit into four:

  • Research and design now overlap instead of running one after the other.
  • 6 interviews (one per persona type) instead of 5–8, and a shorter competitor list.
  • 4 journey maps instead of one per persona: the three helpers share one map.
  • Profile screen and reported content are left for later.
  • Mid-fi covers the core screens, not every screen and state.

If a week slips, protect these three: the decision log, the result page (explored and tested), and the pilot.
